Rods are what is missing, entirely. The foveola is packed with cones and has
essentially zero rod density, so in scotopic light the receptors present are
below threshold and the ones that would work are not there at all. Nothing in
that patch is responding, so there is no weak signal to strain at.

The averted-vision rule is Session 4 restated on the detector side. It helps when
the limit is surface brightness against the sky and does nothing when the limit
is integrated magnitude. M33 spreads magnitude 5.7 over half a degree and sits
barely above background; Uranus packs magnitude 5.64 into four arcseconds, far
above background, so moving it onto coarse rod-sampled retina only costs
resolution.

A hundredfold sensitivity gain is 2.5 log(100), or five magnitudes, against an
observed naked-eye gain of about two from 4 to 6. The shortfall is the sky rather
than the eye: from suburban DFW the moonless background is near 19 mag/as², so
contrast runs out long before rod sensitivity does. At a dark site the gain
approaches the full figure.

Red flashlights, for the public: your night vision lives in cells that take
twenty to thirty minutes to switch on and about a second of white light to switch
back off, and they are nearly blind to deep red. A red light lets you read a
chart and watch your footing without them noticing.

* Concept: three limits, and the loosest one wins
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: concept
:END:

Three things cap the finest detail you can record, and your resolution on a given
night is set by whichever is worst, not by whichever you paid for. Aperture is
the first: diffraction spreads a point into an Airy disk set by wavelength and
aperture alone, and the working rule is the Dawes limit, 116 divided by aperture
in millimetres, in arcseconds. Sampling is the second, since a camera cannot
record detail finer than roughly two pixels. The atmosphere is the third, running
two to four arcseconds here and degrading as about air mass to the 0.6 power,
which ties back to Session 1.

| Limit                      | Value |
|----------------------------+-------|
| Dawes, 50 mm               | 2.32″ |
| Rayleigh, 550 nm           | 2.77″ |
| Pixel scale, 2.9 µm/250 mm | 2.39″ |
| Nyquist sampling floor     | 4.8″  |
| Typical DFW seeing         | 2–4″  |

The Seestar's optics are the best thing in that table and are not what limits it.
At roughly one pixel per Dawes limit it is undersampled, so its practical
resolution is near five arcseconds before the atmosphere takes a share. An
eight-inch scope has a Dawes limit of 0.57″ and never delivers it in Texas.
Aperture buys light, and only sometimes buys detail.

* Exercise: find your own number, then make it worse on purpose
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: exercise
:END:

Epsilon Lyrae is the instrument. Its wide pair is 208″ and splits in anything,
while the two tight components at 2.35″ and 2.4″ sit at the edge of every limit
in the table, so whether they split is a direct readout of the seeing.

** Friday Oct 16

Moon 36% lit and low, setting at 22:58, which costs nothing on stars this bright.

Take Epsilon Lyrae at 20:30 with it 65° up at air mass 1.10, about the best air
you will get, and push magnification until the tight pairs split cleanly, sit
elongated, or stay round. Then walk down the ladder as Perseus climbs: eta Cas at
13.7″ (54° at 22:00), Almach at 9.6″ (44.5°), gamma Arietis at 7.5″ (39.5°). The
finest pair you can still part is your resolution for the night. Return to
Epsilon Lyrae at midnight, down at 25.6° and air mass 2.31 — same optics, same
eye, twice the atmosphere. It should fail, and the failure is the point. Saturn
is at 43° at 22:00 and transits 00:26 at 58°; the Cassini division at 0.7″ will
not resolve in 50 mm on any night, so treat it as a control.

** Saturday Oct 17

First Quarter arrives Sunday at 11:12, so the terminator is near-ideal, the Moon
45% lit and 24° up at 21:00, setting 23:56.

Convert your limit into kilometres. At 402,000 km one arcsecond on the Moon is
1.95 km, so a 2.3″ aperture limit is 4.5 km of surface and the Seestar's sampling
floor is nearer 9.5 km. Find the smallest craterlet you can confidently separate
along the terminator and convert it; the Moon at 24° sits in air mass 2.4, so
expect worse than 4.5 km. After moonset take Almach again at 56°, eleven degrees
higher than Friday, and see whether the altitude changes the verdict.

** Resolution and visual observing

- Arithmetic: 116/50 = 2.32 arcsec; 1.22 × 550 nm / 50 mm = 2.77
  arcsec; 206265 × 0.0029/250 = 2.393 arcsec/pixel. At 402,000 km,
  one arcsecond is 1.949 km, so 2.3 arcsec spans 4.48 km and 4.8
  arcsec spans 9.36 km in the plane of the sky. Lunar foreshortening
  prevents treating that directly as a surface distance near the limb.
- Dawes is an empirical double-star criterion, not the Airy-disk formula.
  The quoted 4.8 arcsec is a two-pixel sampling guideline, not a universal
  hard cutoff. Pixel response, optics, seeing, Bayer sampling and processing
  all matter. Subpixel dithers with reconstruction can recover some sampling
  information. Blurs combine; simply selecting the worst number is a useful
  first approximation, not an exact image-quality model.
- The claims that eight inches never deliver 0.57 arcsec in Texas and buy no
  detail are too absolute. Good seeing, cooling and collimation can permit
  more detail than a 50 mm instrument; no local seeing record is supplied
  to support the stated regional range or an absolute prohibition.
- A failed Epsilon Lyrae split is not a direct measurement of seeing alone:
  aperture, focus, collimation, magnification and observer also contribute.
  The Seestar has no eyepiece; the magnification exercise needs the separate
  eyepiece telescope, whose aperture should be measured rather than assumed.
  Detecting a narrow dark line such as Cassini's division is different from
  resolving two point sources; the claimed guaranteed failure is not a sound
  control experiment. Ring opening and contrast also affect its visibility.
- Averted vision can help faint point sources as well as extended objects.
  Integrated magnitude alone does not rule it out. The quoted hundredfold
  rod/cone sensitivity ratio and five-magnitude conversion do not by
  themselves predict a person's dark-adaptation gain. Red light must be dim;
  a bright red light can still impair adaptation. The original physiological
  simplifications and local sky-brightness claims are not measurements.
