Phase angle is the Sun–object–observer angle, and its vertex sits at the object
being observed, not at your eye and not at the Sun.

The full Moon shows no craters because the phase angle is then near zero. Light
arrives head-on, every shadow falls directly behind the feature that cast it,
and relief disappears. The albedo markings remain, so you still see the maria,
but the topography is invisible.

The extra brightening is the opposition surge, or Seeliger effect. Its cause is
shadow hiding — at near-zero phase the shadows between ring particles are all
concealed behind the particles themselves — reinforced by coherent backscatter.
Saturn's rings show it strongly because a loose particle swarm has a great deal
of shadow available to hide.

A 3:1 shadow-to-height ratio gives tan(alt) = 1/3, so the local solar altitude
is 18.4°.

Rupes Recta is a fault scarp whose steep face looks west. Before local lunar
noon the Sun is in the lunar east, so that face is turned away from the light and
lies in its own shadow: a black line. After lunar noon the Sun has crossed to the
west and shines directly onto the same face: a bright line. The cliff never
changed; the illumination crossed over it.

* Concept: total magnitude lies, surface brightness tells the truth
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: concept
:END:

A catalogue magnitude is the object's *entire* light added up and reported as
though it were a single point. For a star that is a fair description. For
anything extended it is close to useless as a difficulty rating, because what
your eye actually detects is contrast between the object and the sky right next
to it. The relevant quantity is surface brightness: magnitude per square
arcsecond.

Work the two headline objects for tonight:

| Object | Total mag | Apparent size | Surface brightness |
|--------+-----------+---------------+--------------------|
| M31    |       3.4 | 190' × 60'    | about 22.4 mag/as² |
| M57    |       8.8 | 86" × 62"     | about 17.9 mag/as² |

M31 is 5.4 magnitudes brighter in total and about 4.5 magnitudes per square
arcsecond *fainter* on the sky. The catalogue ranking and the observing
difficulty ranking are reversed, and the reason is that M31 spreads its light
over roughly ten thousand times more sky.

Now add the thing you are really fighting. The sky itself has a surface
brightness: roughly 21.9 mag/as² at a genuinely dark site, perhaps 19 from
suburban Dallas-Fort Worth on a moonless night, and brighter still with the Moon
up. Detection happens when the object's surface brightness stands far enough
above the background. Nothing about the object changes when the background rises,
but the contrast collapses.

This also settles a question that confuses people about magnification. Spreading
the image over more area dims an extended object and the sky background in
exactly the same proportion, so the contrast ratio does not improve — you have
made a faint galaxy fainter for nothing. A star is different. It is a point
source, so magnification spreads the *background* out while the star's light
stays concentrated in a near-point image. Contrast against the background
actually improves. That is why magnification is the right tool for splitting
close double stars and the wrong tool for hunting faint galaxies.

* Exercise: the full-Moon contrast test
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: exercise
:END:

Tonight is almost the worst night of the month, which makes it the best night to
prove the point.

** Friday, Sep 25, from 22:00

Take M31 first, at 44° altitude with the Moon 43° away, and then M57 at 65°.
Catalogue magnitudes say M31 should be trivially easier. Record honestly what you
see. The prediction is that the magnitude-8.8 object is the straightforward one
and the magnitude-3.4 object is a struggle.

Then push magnification on M57, and on Epsilon Lyrae, the Double Double. M57
should get dimmer and no easier. Epsilon Lyrae should get better as the
components separate. Same eyepiece changes, opposite outcomes, for the reason in
the concept above.

** Saturday, Sep 26 — the extreme case

Saturn sits 5.7° from the full Moon at 23:10. That is an absurd place to put a
deep-sky object and Saturn will not care at all, because at magnitude 0.4 and a
few arcseconds across its surface brightness is enormously above even
moonlit sky. Then look at M33, which is 28° away from the Moon — four times
further from the glare — and watch it lose. The comparison is the whole lesson in
one pair of pointings.
