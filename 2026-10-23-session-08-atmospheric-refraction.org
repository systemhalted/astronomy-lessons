Your eight-inch gives 116/203, or 0.57 arcseconds, and North Texas never
delivers it. That needs sub-arcsecond zenith seeing with the tube cooled and no
roof plume in the path, where DFW runs two to four and its best nights land
near 1.5. Eight inches buy light, not detail.

One pixel per resolution element cannot represent a feature; it takes two
across it before a detector records a feature rather than one brighter square.
So the Seestar's 2.39-arcsecond pixels put its real floor near 4.8 arcseconds,
twice its own optical limit, and detail between the two figures is gathered by
the optics and discarded by the sensor.

Air mass at 25 degrees is 1/sin 25, or 2.37, and that to the 0.6 power is 1.65,
so 2.5 arcseconds at the zenith becomes 4.1. Epsilon Lyrae's tight pairs are
2.35 and 2.4 arcseconds, well inside that blur, so the midnight failure was
predictable before you looked.

For a visitor: a bigger mirror collects more light and shows fainter things,
but sharpness depends on the air the light came through, and in Texas the air
is almost always the limit.

* Concept: the atmosphere lifts everything, and lifts the bottom more
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: concept
:END:

Session 1 covered what a slant path takes away and Session 7 what it does to
fine detail. The third thing it does is bend the whole beam, so every object
appears higher than it geometrically is — about 34 arcminutes of lift at the
horizon, falling off roughly as the cotangent of altitude, which makes it
larger than the Moon's own diameter down low and nothing at all overhead.

Two consequences matter in the field. Published rise and set times already
include this, so at the moment of sunset the Sun's whole disk is geometrically
below the horizon and you are watching an object that has already set. And the
lift is not uniform across a disk: the lower limb sits in thicker air and rises
more than the upper, so a low Moon is squashed vertically while its width is
untouched. Refraction is wavelength-dependent too, bending blue about 1.3
percent more than red, which smears a low target into a short vertical colour
streak.

| Centre altitude | Refraction lift | Vertical squash on a 32' disk | Blue–red spread |
|-----------------+-----------------+-------------------------------+-----------------|
| 0°              | 34.0'           | 6.5' (20%)                    | 22"             |
| 2°              | 17.4'           | 2.4' (7.5%)                   | 14"             |
| 5°              | 10.2'           | 0.84' (2.6%)                  | 7.5"            |
| 10°             | 5.3'            | 0.27' (0.8%)                  | 4.1"            |
| 20°             | 2.7'            | 0.08'                         | 2.1"            |
| 45°             | 1.0'            | 0.02'                         | 0.8"            |
| 60°             | 0.6'            | 0.01'                         | 0.4"            |

* Exercise: measure the squash, then time the lift
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: exercise
:END:

** Friday Oct 23

A near-full Moon rising due east is the best refraction target of the year, and
at 2.39 arcseconds per pixel the Seestar makes the effect countable: the disk
is about 800 pixels wide and the predicted squash at two degrees is 59 of them.
Moonrise is 17:12 at azimuth 87 degrees, well before sunset at 18:44, so this
is a daylight shoot. Take frames at 17:15 (centre on the horizon, predicted
vertical/horizontal ratio 0.79), 17:25 (2.1 degrees, 0.925) and 17:45 (6.2
degrees, 0.982), then one at 21:00 at 44.7 degrees as the round control, and
plot ratio against altitude. At 18:45 Saturn is 10.6 degrees up and 8.6 degrees
from the Moon: shoot it for the colour spread, about 4 arcseconds, and again at
22:00 at 48 degrees where it is a fifth of that. Saturn transits 23:57 at 58.3
degrees.

** Saturday Oct 24

Time the lift instead of measuring it. The leading limb clears a flat horizon
at 17:39, the almanac moonrise is 17:41, and the Moon's centre does not
geometrically reach the horizon until 17:44, so five minutes of what you watch
is an object that is not there yet. Pick a flat eastern view at azimuth 79
degrees and catch first light with a stopwatch. Then repeat the squash
measurement at 17:55 (2.2 degrees, 0.928) and 18:05 (4.2 degrees, 0.967), and
close with the control at 23:00 when the Moon is 61.4 degrees up and Saturn
55.9.

** Worked answers and refraction model

- The seeing arithmetic should read (1/sin 25 degrees)^0.6 = 1.677,
  giving 4.19 arcsec, or 4.2 arcsec rather than 4.1. The eight-inch and
  sampling qualifications in Session 7's review apply here too. No recorded
  midnight observation is supplied, so the answer's failure is a prediction.
- Refraction depends on pressure, temperature, wavelength and atmospheric
  structure. The cotangent approximation is about R = 1 arcmin × cot(h)
  away from the horizon; it diverges at zero altitude and cannot predict
  horizon refraction. The table does not specify a model, atmospheric
  conditions, or whether h is true or apparent altitude. Its near-horizon
  flattening numbers are therefore illustrative, not calibrated predictions.
- A constant 1.3 percent colour difference needs specified wavelengths.
  At the table's 34 arcmin horizon lift it would imply 26.5 arcsec, not
  22 arcsec. Without wavelength endpoints the dispersion column is not
  independently reproducible. Horizontal width is approximately unchanged
  in a small-field, vertically stratified model, not exactly invariant in
  every real atmosphere.
- Almanac moonrise already means the apparent upper limb meets the ideal
  horizon (whether that limb is illuminated depends on phase). Do not add
  another earlier upper-limb event to the published time. Thus 17:39 versus
  17:41 is not explained by the stated geometry. Terrain, weather and the
  bright limb can shift what an observer actually detects.
- The 803 by 770 pixel question is hypothetical. Its 4.11 percent flattening
  is correct, but a unique altitude needs a specified refraction model and
  uncertainty. Linear interpolation between the source table's 2 and 5
  degree rows gives about 4.1 degrees, not 3.5. That interpolation is itself
  only approximate because the relationship is nonlinear; Session 9's
  18:01 answer must not be read as a measured timestamp.

** Historical sky data

Using the PyEphem conventions documented in Session 7's review gives upper-limb
moonrise about 17:10 CDT on Oct 23 and 17:40 CDT on Oct 24, compared with
17:12 and 17:41 in the source. Atmospheric and horizon conventions matter at
this precision. Sunset, dusk, the listed 21:00 lunar altitudes, Saturn's 22:00
altitudes and its transit times broadly agree. The source's lunar diameters
lack a reference time and topocentric/geocentric convention; these vary through
the night, so their quoted hundredth-arcminute precision is not justified.
USNO places Full Moon on Oct 25 at 23:12 CDT, one minute after the source.
At a distance of 363,000 km a 3,475 km lunar diameter subtends about 32.9
arcmin. Perigee timing and the original exact double-star separations have not
been independently verified in this migration. All original figures remain
above as historical predictions, not actual observations.
