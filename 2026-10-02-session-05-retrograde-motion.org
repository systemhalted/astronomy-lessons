The 10' cluster is easier. Both deliver the same total light, but the compact one
concentrates it into a thirty-sixth of the sky area, so its surface brightness is
about 3.9 mag/as² higher and it stands that much further above the moonlit
background.

Magnification helps a double star because a star is a point source: enlarging the
image spreads the sky background out while the stellar images stay essentially
point-like, so contrast improves and the pair separates. A galaxy is extended, so
magnification dims object and background in the same proportion, leaving contrast
unchanged and the view merely fainter.

A full Moon costs roughly 3 mag/as² at a genuinely dark site, taking the sky from
about 21.9 to about 18.9. From suburban Dallas-Fort Worth, where the moonless sky
is already near 19, it costs far less — perhaps 1 to 1.5 magnitudes — because
light pollution has already spent most of that budget. The counterintuitive
corollary is that moonlight hurts dark-site observers much more than it hurts us.

Public version: Saturn is tiny and intensely bright, so it packs its light into a
few arcseconds and swamps anything the Moon does to the sky around it. M31 spreads
a similar amount of light across an area bigger than your fist at arm's length, so
the moonlit sky is brighter than the galaxy is and there is nothing left to see.

* Concept: retrograde motion is your own sideways velocity, projected
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: concept
:END:

Outer planets periodically stop their eastward march against the stars, back up
westward for weeks, stop again and resume. There is nothing happening at the
planet. What you are seeing is Earth's own transverse velocity projected onto the
sky, and the moment it peaks is opposition.

At opposition, Earth and the outer planet are lined up on the same side of the
Sun and both are moving in the same direction, perpendicular to your line of
sight. Only the difference in speed matters:

: Earth   29.8 km/s
: Saturn   9.6 km/s
: relative 20.2 km/s transverse

Run that through one day at Saturn's opposition distance of 8.434 AU:

: 20.2 km/s × 86400 s        = 1.745 × 10^6 km of sideways displacement
: 8.434 AU                   = 1.262 × 10^9 km
: 1.745e6 / 1.262e9          = 1.383 × 10^-3 rad
: × 206265                   = 285 arcsec/day

The ephemeris gives 283″/day. The 2″ residual is orbital eccentricity — neither
body is travelling at its circular-orbit mean speed on this particular date.

Note which term dominates. Earth supplies the entire 29.8 km/s and Saturn's own
orbital motion claws back only about a third of it. Retrograde motion is
overwhelmingly a statement about us, not about the planet.

* Exercise: measure 283 arcseconds per day with the Seestar
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: exercise
:END:

The predicted rate is 283″/day, which is 4.7′ per night and 32.9′ over seven
nights. That last figure is about the diameter of the full Moon, so it is well
within reach of a wide field.

Frame Saturn deliberately *off-centre* against the Pisces field stars, so there is
room for it to travel across the frame rather than out of it. Capture on Friday
Oct 2, Saturday Oct 3 and again the following Friday. Keep the framing as close to
identical as you can manage — the field stars are your fixed reference, not the
frame edge.

For an in-frame ruler, use Saturn's rings. The ring span is about 45″, so a
night's motion is roughly six ring-spans. You can state the result as a multiple
of something visible in the same image, which is a far more convincing
demonstration at a public event than a number you assert.

Continue the Saturn brightness log from Session 3 through opposition night, which
is where the Seeliger surge should show up in your own data.
