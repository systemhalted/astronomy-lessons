At opposition Earth and Saturn both move the same direction across the line of
sight, so only the difference counts: 29.8 − 9.6 = 20.2 km/s. Over a day that is
1.745 × 10^6 km of sideways displacement, and at 8.434 AU (1.262 × 10^9 km) it
subtends 1.383 × 10^-3 rad, or 285″. Earth's motion supplies all of it and
Saturn's own orbital speed claws back only about a third. The 2″ gap against the
ephemeris is eccentricity.

Mars beats Saturn on 5.7 km/s because angular rate is speed divided by distance,
and Mars at opposition is near 0.5 AU against Saturn's 8.4:

: Mars   5.7 / 0.52  ->  about 1300 arcsec/day
: Saturn 20.2 / 8.43 ->  about  285 arcsec/day

About four and a half times faster. The distance divisor outweighs the velocity
numerator by more than the velocity deficit costs.

The missing 2.75° is Saturn's ecliptic latitude: −2.71° geocentric, −2.43°
heliocentric. Opposition is defined in ecliptic *longitude* alone, and on Oct 4
that longitude difference closed to 0.008°, but Saturn still sat two and a half
degrees below Earth's orbital plane. Project Earth's 1 AU offset perpendicular to
the Sun–Saturn line and divide:

: 1 AU × sin(2.43°) = 0.042 AU
: 0.042 / 8.434      = 5.03 × 10^-3 rad = 0.29°

So the brightness surge peaked near zero phase, not at it.

Public version of the conjunction question: retrograde happens when Earth
overtakes a planet on the same side of the Sun, the way a car you pass seems to
slide backwards. Near conjunction the planet is across the Sun from us, so we
never pass it; our orbital motion swings our sightline the same direction the
planet is already drifting, and the two add to give the fastest eastward motion of
the whole cycle.

* Concept: the sensitive detector has a hole in the middle
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: concept
:END:

Cones fill the fovea, the central degree or so of your vision. They give you
colour and fine detail and they need roughly a hundred times more light than rods
do. Rods carry none of that information — they are achromatic and coarse — but
they are far more sensitive, their density peaks about 15 to 20 degrees off the
fovea, and there are essentially none at the very centre. The most sensitive part
of your retina is not where you are pointing it.

Rods also take time. Most of the gain arrives in twenty to thirty minutes, full
adaptation closer to forty-five, and one glance at a phone screen resets it. Their
peak response shifts bluer as well, near 500 nm against the cones' 555 nm, which
is why faint nebulosity reads grey-green and why red light preserves adaptation.

Session 4 was about the target: surface brightness against sky background. This is
about the detector, and it changes which objects respond to technique.

* Exercise: the same magnitude, two different detectors
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: exercise
:END:

| Object | Total mag | Extent | Detector required |
|--------+-----------+--------+-------------------|
| M33    |       5.7 | 0.5°   | rods, off-axis    |
| Uranus |      5.64 | < 4"   | cones, direct     |

Identical brightness totals, opposite requirements. Observe both and record which
one averted vision helps.

** Friday Oct 9

Moon one percent lit and set at 18:20, sunset 19:01, astronomical dark 20:23.
Start adapting by 21:45 with no white light.

At 22:00 take M31 at 54° in the east, then M33 at 42°, using Mirach 7.1° away as
the pointer. Centre M33 in binoculars and then deliberately shift your gaze to the
field edge, which puts it 25 to 30 degrees off-axis apparent. Note whether it
appears or strengthens. Repeat at 23:00 when M33 has climbed to 55°.

At 00:00 take Uranus, by then 31° up in the east at air mass 1.9, and try the same
trick. It should do nothing, which is the result you want.

** Saturday Oct 10

New Moon at 10:50 that morning, moonset 18:48, dark at 20:22.

Map your own rod peak on M33 at three gaze offsets — field centre, halfway out,
hard edge — and see which wins, then compare against the 15-to-20-degree figure.

At 22:00 split Albireo at 60° for gold and blue, then find a faint star in the same
field and confirm it shows no colour at all. That is rods doing the work.

The Double Cluster climbs from 39° at 22:00 to 55° by midnight and is a good
scotopic-shift target.

Save Saturn for last, 49° at 23:00 and transiting at 00:55 at 59°, because at
magnitude 0.4 it will cost you several minutes of adaptation.
