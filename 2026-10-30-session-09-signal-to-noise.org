The cotangent rule gives 11.4′ at 5° against the table's 10.2′, and 1.00′ at
45° against 1.0′, so it is exact where the lift hardly matters and worst where
it does. At published sunset the upper limb is apparently on the horizon, so it
is 34′ down geometrically and the centre a semidiameter further, 50′ in all,
about 1.6 solar diameters.

Refraction displaces an object along the vertical only, by an amount set by
altitude alone. The lower limb looks through a longer slant path than the upper
and is lifted more, so the disk loses height. The left and right limbs sit at
equal altitude, pass through identical air and are lifted identically in the
same direction, so the width never changes.

A disk 803 pixels wide and 770 tall is 4.1 percent squashed, between the 2° and
5° rows and nearer the latter, so about 3.5°. The Moon passed 2.2° at 17:55 and
4.2° at 18:05 on Saturday, putting that frame near 18:01. For a visitor: the
air bends light downward and lifts the low Sun into view by roughly its own
width, so the printed time is when you can still see it rather than when it is
really there.

* Concept: four times the time for twice the result
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: concept
:END:

Target photons accumulate in proportion to total integration, but the noise
that buries them accumulates as its square root, since the dominant term under
suburban skies is photon noise in the background. Signal-to-noise therefore
goes as the square root of time, and the consequence is that quadrupling the
exposure only doubles the contrast. The depth gained is 1.25 times the
logarithm of the time factor, so a factor of ten buys 1.25 magnitudes, less
than the 1.5 that doubling aperture gives.

Background enters as a divisor, so the cost of a bright sky is linear: twice
the background needs twice the integration for the same result. A gibbous Moon
does not hide a galaxy, it taxes the clock, and the tax is a multiplier you can
measure. Stacking never touches Session 7's resolution limit, and it stops
helping once gradients and fixed-pattern terms dominate, because those do not
average down.

** Friday Oct 30

Dark begins 20:01 and the 69 percent Moon rises 22:46, giving 165 moonless
minutes and then a controlled change in the sky. NGC 7331 in Pegasus, magnitude
9.5, transits 21:27 at 88.6° and holds above 80° from 20:45 to 22:15 at air
mass under 1.02, which removes altitude from the experiment. Run 10-second subs
continuously from 20:45 to 23:30 and do the work afterwards on that one
dataset. Sub-stack the moonless part into 5, 10, 20, 40 and 80-minute totals,
measure signal-to-noise on the outer disk against adjacent sky, and check it
against the table. Then compare the background rate per second per pixel near
22:30, Moon 2° below the horizon, with the subs near 23:20, Moon 6° up and 103°
away: that ratio is your lunar tax. The galaxy also fell from 80° to 65°, which
raises background on its own, so treat the ratio as an upper bound.

** Saturday Oct 31

Dark at 20:00 and no Moon until 23:57 gives 237 minutes, so run the curve
forward instead of measuring it again. M33 climbs from 53° at 21:30 to 81° at
23:45. Build a 10-minute stack early, pick the faintest field star you would
call a detection, and predict its counterpart in the full 135-minute stack:
13.5 times the time, 1.41 magnitudes deeper. Plate-solve and check that against
catalogue magnitudes rather than the screen. The next 1.4 magnitudes costs
another factor of 13.5, or thirty hours, which is why faint-object work is
counted in nights. Close on Saturn, 57.6° at 23:00.

** Signal-to-noise and calculations

- For source rate s, sky rate b, dark rate d (all in the same measurement
  aperture), total time t, N frames, and read-noise variance r^2 per frame,
  SNR = s t / sqrt((s + b + d)t + N r^2), before extra background-estimation
  and systematic terms. With fixed subexposure length and stable conditions,
  SNR scales as sqrt(t). In the sky-dominated limit it scales as sqrt(t/b).
  Twice the sky rate then needs twice the time for the same SNR.
- Four times the time doubles SNR, not the object's physical contrast against
  the sky. The positive limiting-magnitude gain is 1.25 log10(t2/t1).
  All integration-table counts, SNR ratios and rounded magnitude gains check
  out. The background table gives positive brightness differences; a brighter
  sky has a numerically smaller mag/arcsec^2 value.
- 135/10 = 13.5 and 1.25 log10(13.5) = 1.413 mag. Another factor 13.5
  takes the total to 1,822.5 minutes = 30.4 hours, rather than thirty
  additional hours. This assumes unchanged sky, throughput and detection
  threshold. Doubling aperture's 1.5-mag collecting-area gain is not a
  general sky-limited imaging-depth comparison; angular measurement area,
  focal ratio, image scale and PSF must also be specified.
- Ordinary averaging does not beat diffraction or seeing. Dithered subpixel
  reconstruction can improve undersampling, so stacking never changes
  resolution is too broad. Calibration, dithering and gradient modelling
  can reduce some systematic terms; they need not impose an immutable floor.
- The Friday before/after background ratio mixes Moon, air mass, transparency
  and instrumental changes. It is a confounded comparison, not a guaranteed
  upper bound on the Moon's contribution. Subtract bias/dark consistently,
  use linear un-stretched data and identical measurement apertures, retain
  accepted subframes, and report elapsed versus actual accepted exposure.
  A Seestar run near the zenith may also have alt-az tracking and field-rotation
  limitations, so the planned 10-second frames are not guaranteed usable.
- Session 8's refraction-model and hypothetical lunar-image qualifications
  apply to the worked answers here. Standard sunset places the solar centre
  about 50 arcmin below the horizon (about 1.6 diameters); the 34 arcmin
  refraction shift alone is about one diameter. The cotangent rule is an
  approximation, including at 45 degrees, rather than exact physics.
