Your hand span is whatever you measured, and the baseline you checked it against
was Polaris at 33.15° above the northern horizon.

The Polaris rule, in public-facing form: the height of the North Star above your
horizon is your latitude, so the same star that tells you which way is north
also tells you how far north you are.

Air mass at 30° is 1/sin(30°) = 2.00 exactly. It means you are looking through
twice as much atmosphere as you would be if the object were straight overhead.

Low objects redden because the longer air path scatters short wavelengths out of
the beam far more efficiently than long ones. Blue light gets removed, red light
survives, and what reaches your eye is what is left. It is the same mechanism as
a red sunset, applied to a star.

* Concept: the sky runs on a different clock than you do
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: concept
:END:

A solar day is 24 hours: the time for the Sun to return to the same place in
your sky. A sidereal day is the time for a *star* to return to the same place,
and it is shorter:

: sidereal day  = 23h 56m 04.09s  (86164.09 s)
: solar day     = 24h 00m 00.00s  (86400.00 s)
: difference    =      3m 55.91s  (235.91 s)

The difference exists because Earth is doing two things at once. It spins, and
it also moves about one degree further along its orbit each day. After one full
rotation relative to the stars, Earth has to turn roughly one extra degree to
bring the Sun back overhead, and that extra degree takes just under four
minutes. So the sidereal day is the true rotation period and the solar day is
the rotation period plus an orbital correction.

The practical consequence is the one worth internalising. Every star, cluster
and galaxy rises, transits and sets about *3 minutes 56 seconds earlier each
night* by your wall clock. Round it to four minutes. Over a week that is half an
hour. Over a month it is two hours. This is the whole reason the constellations
turn over through the year, and it is also a prediction tool: if you know where
something was at a given clock time tonight, you know where it will be at that
same clock time any night you care to name.

The companion figure: four minutes of clock time corresponds to very close to
one degree of sky rotation (360° / 1436 min × 4 min = 1.003°). So the nightly
drift is one degree of sky per night at a fixed clock time.

* Exercise: measure the drift, then invert it

** Part one: two consecutive nights

Pick a fixed terrestrial landmark — a roofline, a particular tree, a utility
pole — something you can stand in exactly the same spot to use. Watch M31 and
record the clock time at which it sits directly above that landmark. Do it again
the following night from the same spot.

The second time should be about four minutes earlier. One night of difference is
uncomfortably close to your own timing error, which is exactly why part two
matters.

** Part two: stretch the baseline to eight nights

Four minutes is hard to measure. Thirty-two minutes is easy. The prediction for
this run was:

| Date   | Predicted time over landmark |
|--------+------------------------------|
| Sep 11 | 21:00                        |
| Sep 19 | 20:28                        |

That is 32 minutes over 8 nights, or 4.0 minutes per night. Stretching the
baseline divides your timing error by the number of nights, which is the general
trick for measuring any slow rate with crude equipment.

** Part three: run it backwards

Prediction is the real payoff. Saturn reached 27° altitude at a particular clock
time on the night of Sep 11. Using the drift rule, work out when it next returns
to 27°:

: shift = N nights × 3m 55.91s

For three weeks out, 21 × 235.91 s = 4954 s = 82.6 minutes, so an object that
hit a given altitude at 22:00 tonight hits it at about 20:37 twenty-one nights
from now. Then go outside and check it.
