* Concept: the two numbers that locate anything in your sky
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: concept
:END:

Every object you point at has two coordinates in the frame that actually
matters when you are standing in a field: altitude, the angle above the
horizon, and azimuth, the compass bearing measured clockwise from north. Right
ascension and declination are fixed to the sky and are what catalogues use, but
they tell you nothing about whether a thing is observable tonight from your
driveway. Altitude and azimuth change continuously, they are specific to your
location and the moment, and they are what decides whether an object is worth
setting up for.

Altitude matters more than azimuth, because altitude sets how much atmosphere
you are looking through. That quantity is called air mass, and to a good
approximation it is the cosecant of the altitude:

: air mass = 1 / sin(altitude)

| Altitude | Air mass |
|----------+----------|
|      90° |     1.00 |
|      60° |     1.15 |
|      45° |     1.41 |
|      40° |     1.56 |
|      30° |     2.00 |
|      20° |     2.92 |
|      15° |     3.86 |
|      10° |     5.76 |

At the zenith you look through one atmosphere. At 30° you look through two. At
10° you look through nearly six, and the image is being dragged through all of
it. This is why the single most useful observing habit is to wait for altitude
rather than fight for it.

Air mass costs you three separate things. It dims the object, because more
atmosphere absorbs and scatters more light. It reddens the object, because
Rayleigh scattering removes short wavelengths far more efficiently than long
ones, which is the same physics that makes a sunset red. And it destroys
resolution, because every layer of turbulent air along the path smears the
image, so seeing at low altitude is always worse than seeing overhead on the
same night.

* Exercise: calibrate your hand, then prove the air mass penalty
:PROPERTIES:
:CUSTOM_ID: exercise
:END:

The point of this one is to give you an instrument you cannot leave at home.

** Part one: calibrate your hand span

Hold your arm out straight and spread your fingers. The span from thumb tip to
little finger tip, at full arm's length, covers somewhere between 20 and 25
degrees for most adults. Yours is yours and you need the actual number.

Find Polaris. Its altitude above your northern horizon equals your
geographic latitude, which from the Dallas-Fort Worth area is 33.15°, and this
is exact enough for fieldwork because Polaris sits within a degree of the true
pole. So you have a known 33° angle standing in your sky for free, every clear
night, all year. Stack hand spans from the horizon up to Polaris and count. If
it takes a span and a half, your span is about 22°. Write the number down and
use it for the rest of the series.

While you are there, calibrate the smaller units too: a fist at arm's length is
roughly 10°, three middle fingers roughly 5°, the little fingernail roughly 1°.
Check each against the Polaris baseline rather than trusting the textbook
figure.

** Part two: the same object, twice

Pick Saturn and observe it twice on the same night, once low in the east
shortly after it clears the horizon and once near the meridian hours later.
Same telescope, same eyepiece, same eye, same target. Note each time what you
can and cannot see: ring separation, the Cassini division if conditions allow,
the banding on the globe, and the colour of the globe itself.

The low view will be dimmer, distinctly more yellow-orange, and mushy. The high
view will be sharper and whiter. Nothing about Saturn changed in those hours.
The entire difference is the column of air you were looking through, and the air
mass table tells you how much of it there was.

Doing this once, deliberately, is what turns the table above from a fact you
know into a habit you have.
